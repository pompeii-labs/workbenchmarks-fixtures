Should You Put Solar Panels on Your Roof to Power Your Sauna?

An 8 kW sauna heater sounds ravenous, and roof panels feel like the cleanest way to run it guilt-free. Follow the kilowatt-hours and cash flows across a day, a year, and 25 years—and test every assumption yourself.

Solar Data: US DOE / NLR PVWatts v8 (NSRDB TMY-2020 · Station #778153 · 1,397.6 kWh/kW-yr) Array: Fixed Roof Mount · 180° Due South · 35° Tilt · 14% Losses Tax Law: Spring 2027 Homeowner Purchase (IRC § 25D = 0%)

The Bottom Line

Almost certainly no—with one narrow net-metering loophole if you build tiny and skip the battery.

1. An 8 kW heater is a big power draw, not a big electric bill. Used every Tuesday, Thursday, Saturday, and Sunday at 6:30 pm (45 minutes at 8 kW to heat up, 45 minutes at 4 kW to bathe), the sauna consumes 9.0 kWh per evening—1,872 kWh a year. At $0.16/kWh, status-quo grid power costs $299.52 in Year 1 ($1.44 a bath), or $5,716 in present value across all 25 years. That sets a hard ceiling on how much capital a sauna-only project can justify.

2. 6:30 pm misses the sun, and the 30% federal tax credit is gone. Under Public Law 119-21 (July 2025), the residential solar credit (IRC § 25D) expired December 31, 2025, so a Spring 2027 buyer pays the full $3.00/W. Meanwhile, south-facing panels generate ~99% of their power before the heater switches on. Under net billing ($0.04/kWh exports), panels never pay back at any size; adding an $11,000 battery (+$6,000 in Year 13) loses over $13,000 in NPV and still pulls grid power during every 8 kW preheat because the battery maxes out at 5 kW.

3. The sole exception: Under 1:1 annual net metering with no battery, a micro-array of 0.5 to 2.15 kW (peaking near 1.42 kW, ~3–4 panels, +$1,421 NPV / 7.86% IRR) clears a 5% hurdle—not by physically heating your stones, but by using the utility meter as a free seasonal bank, provided an installer will actually build a four-panel roof system at $3.00/W.

Verdict: No 3.00 kW · Net Metering · No Battery

Part I · Across a Single Day

Why a 6:30 pm Sauna Almost Never Meets a South-Facing Sun

Start with the clock. In Minneapolis (44.98° N, 93.27° W), an unshaded roof array facing due south at a 35° tilt peaks around 12:15 pm in winter (CST) and 1:15 pm during Daylight Saving Time (CDT). Your sauna, however, sits cold all afternoon and switches on at 6:30 pm on the wall clock, drawing 8.0 kW for 45 minutes (6.0 kWh) to heat the rocks and averaging 4.0 kW for 45 minutes (3.0 kWh) while you bathe, before shutting off at 8:00 pm.

From November through mid-March, sunset in the Twin Cities falls between 4:31 pm and 6:15 pm—your panels produce 0.00 kW when the contactor closes. Even in late June, when sunset stretches past 9:00 pm CDT, by 6:30 pm the sun has swung into the northwest, glancing obliquely across a due-south roof. At your current 3.00 kW array size, direct panel-to-heater electricity supplies just 24.8 kWh out of 1,872 kWh all year (1.3%).

What if you add the optional 13.5 kWh battery? Toggle it on above and watch the 6:30 pm bar in Figure 1. Because the battery inverter's maximum discharge power is 5.0 kW while the heater demands 8.0 kW for the first 45 minutes, a physical 3.0 kW power gap (~2.25 kWh per session) spills onto the utility grid even on a clear July evening when the battery starts 100% full.

Part II · Across a Year

Where the Kilowatt-Hours Go—and Why Tariff Rules Decide Their Value

Zoom out to all 365 days of Year 1. Across 52 weeks of 2027, there are exactly 208 sauna evenings (52 Tuesdays, Thursdays, Saturdays, and Sundays; Jan 1 and Dec 31 fall on Fridays), demanding 1,872.0 kWh at a steady pace of 144 to 162 kWh every month. Meanwhile, your 3.00 kW south-facing array produces 4,193 kWh—ranging from abundant spring and summer sun down to snow-muted December, when output is less than half of July's.

Because 4,168 kWh leaves your house to the grid while 1,847 kWh enters from the grid at night, the entire economics of solar-for-a-sauna turn on how your utility settles that two-way traffic:

Under Net Metering, every exported kilowatt-hour erases one imported kilowatt-hour over the year at the full $0.16 retail rate, up to the sauna's use; only annual surplus beyond it earns $0.04/kWh. Notice a counterintuitive consequence in Table 1: under net metering, adding a battery actually lowers your Year 1 bill savings, because running electricity through a 90%-efficient chemical battery burns 10% of the energy as heat instead of banking it 100% loss-free on the utility's ledger! Under Net Billing, by contrast, every exported kWh is paid $0.04 while every evening import costs full retail—crushing solar-only savings by ~75%.

Part III · Across the 25-Year Life of the System

Full-Freight Capital Costs, Panel Degradation, and the 1.42 kW Kink

Why is upfront capital recovery so unforgiving in Spring 2027? For four years (2022–2025), homeowners took a 30% federal tax credit for granted. Not anymore:

Statutory Note — Why the Spring 2027 Federal Tax Credit Is $0: Under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Public Law 119-21, 139 Stat. 72, enacted July 4, 2025; IRS Fact Sheet FS-2025-05), Congress terminated the IRC § 25D Residential Clean Energy Credit for any solar or battery property placed in service after December 31, 2025. A homeowner purchasing a 3.00 kW system in Spring 2027 receives $0 in federal tax credits and pays the full $9,000 out of pocket. (Had the 30% credit survived, it would have shaved $2,700 off Year 0 and shifted your NPV to +$1,018.)

Over 25 years, two opposing clocks run simultaneously: both grid and export electricity prices compound upward by 3.0% per year from Year 2 onward, while crystalline-silicon panel output degrades by 0.5% per year (producing 88.67% of Year 1 kWh by Year 25). Notice in Figure 4 how discounting future dollars at 5.0% separates nominal break-even from true economic value: an array can eventually cross zero in nominal Year-18 dollars while still leaving you poorer in present value than putting your Year 0 cash in a 5% Treasury or index account.

Now look at Figure 5, which plots 25-year NPV across every array size from 0.5 kW to 10.0 kW. Under Net Metering · Solar Only (green curve), every kilowatt up to 1.34 kW offsets 100% retail electricity in Year 1 ($223.62/yr per $3,000 kW, a 7.86% IRR). Accounting for 0.5%/yr panel fade over 25 years, NPV peaks sharply at 1.42 kW. Past that elbow, every extra kilowatt of panels costs $3,000 up front only to export lifetime surplus at $0.04/kWh ($55.90/yr in Year 1)—destroying ~$1,990 of NPV per extra kilowatt and flipping the entire project negative above 2.15 kW.

Part IV · Sensitivity Analysis

What Would Have to Change to Flip the Verdict?

Good financial analysis doesn't stop at a point estimate; it tells you where the fences sit. Holding your other active inputs fixed, here are the exact thresholds required for your current configuration to break even (NPV = $0), and why the battery fails by such a wide margin even under Net Billing:

Under Net Billing at $0.16/kWh retail and $0.04/kWh export, cycling solar through a 90%-efficient battery instead of exporting 1.111 kWh at $0.0444 yields a net arbitrage spread of $0.1156 per delivered kWh. Because the sauna's 8 kW preheat exceeds the battery's 5 kW cap, the battery can deliver at most ~1,392 kWh/yr to the sauna—worth only ~$161/yr in Year 1 arbitrage (~$3,080 in 25-year present value at 5%). Yet the battery plus its Year-13 replacement costs $14,182 in present value ($11,000 + $6,000 ÷ 1.0513). You are spending $14,182 to capture $3,080.

One real-world caveat before you close the book: This model obeys the crucial capital-budgeting rule of isolation—evaluating an array bought strictly for the sauna, treating the rest of the house as off-limits. In real life, a homeowner's main breaker panel serves refrigerators, air conditioning, Dishwashers, and heat pumps during the middle of the day when solar peaks. An array that loses money under Net Billing against an evening-only sauna can look dramatically better when daytime kitchen and cooling loads absorb $0.16/kWh power directly on the spot—or if you preheat a well-insulated sauna at 4:30 pm on summer weekends instead of 6:30 pm.
