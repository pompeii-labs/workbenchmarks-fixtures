From sunlight
to current

Every second, about four thousand billion billion particles of light land on each square metre of a sunny rooftop. A solar cell is a thin slice of purified silicon that catches many of them and turns them into a steady flow of electrons in a wire.

This is how it works, told by following a single photon from the Sun to the socket.

Begin the trip ↓

1 · Departure

A glow from 150 million kilometres away

Our photon's energy was released deep in the Sun's core, where hydrogen nuclei fuse into helium. That energy takes a remarkably long time to get out. The interior is so dense that light is absorbed and re-emitted countless times on the way up, a journey estimated at tens of thousands of years or more. By the time the energy reaches the visible surface, the photosphere, it has become the glow of very hot gas at about 5,500 °C.

Our photon leaves from there. Space is nearly empty, so nothing gets in its way, and it crosses the 150 million kilometres to Earth in 8 minutes and 20 seconds.

Anything hot glows, and the hotter it is, the further its glow shifts from red toward blue. A stove ring glows dull red and a lamp filament yellow-white. The Sun's surface is hotter still, so its light spans the whole rainbow and spills well beyond it at both ends. The chart shows how the Sun's power is spread across colours.

Above the atmosphere, each square metre facing the Sun receives about 1,361 watts. At the ground on a clear day, after the air has scattered and absorbed some of it, the standard figure used to rate solar panels is 1,000 watts per square metre. That is roughly the power of a microwave oven per square metre. More than half of it is infrared, which our eyes can't see. A silicon solar cell can use some of that infrared, but not all of it. To see why, we need to look at light in a different way.

2 · The packet

Light arrives in packets

Light travels as a wave, but it hands over its energy in indivisible packets called photons. The energy of a photon depends only on its colour: the shorter the wavelength, the bigger the packet. A blue photon carries more energy than a red one, and an infrared photon less still.

These energies are tiny, so physicists measure them in electron-volts (eV), the energy an electron picks up when it is pushed through one volt, like crossing a 1-volt battery. A photon of green light carries about 2.3 eV.

This is the key idea of the whole story: a photon is all or nothing. Bright red light and dim red light differ only in how many photons arrive, not in what each one can do. A material either accepts a photon's packet whole or doesn't take it at all. For silicon, a packet must carry at least 1.12 eV, which corresponds to near-infrared light at 1,107 nm. Photons with less energy are not absorbed, no matter how many there are.

3 · Arrival

Inside a crystal of silicon

Most solar cells are made of silicon, the element that makes up most of ordinary sand. For a cell, it is refined until impurities are counted in parts per million or less, then grown into a single, perfectly ordered crystal.

Each silicon atom has four outer electrons. In the crystal, every atom shares them with four neighbours, so each bond holds a shared pair. The result is a rigid three-dimensional framework with the same layout as diamond. Because every outer electron is locked into a bond, none is free to move, and pure silicon in the dark barely conducts electricity.

When silicon absorbs a photon with enough energy, the photon knocks one electron out of its bond. That electron is now free to roam through the crystal. Just as important is what it leaves behind: an empty spot in a bond, called a hole. A neighbouring electron can hop into that spot, which moves the hole the other way. So the hole wanders too, and behaves just like a free positive charge.

One photon, one free electron, one hole: an electron–hole pair.

Left alone, the pair doesn't last. Sooner or later, typically within microseconds to milliseconds, a wandering electron meets a hole and drops back into a bond, giving up its energy as heat or a faint glow. Everything clever about a solar cell is a way to pull electrons and holes apart before that happens.

4 · The toll

An energy ladder with a missing rung

Physicists usually draw the same event as a ladder of energies. Electrons locked in bonds sit on the lower rungs, called the valence band, which is completely full. Free electrons sit on the upper rungs, the conduction band, which is empty in the dark. Between them is a stretch of ladder with no rungs at all: the band gap. An electron cannot sit there, so it can only cross by taking one big jump of at least the full gap.

This gives two separate ways to waste sunlight. Photons below the gap are not absorbed at all: they pass straight through, as if the silicon were glass. Photons far above the gap are absorbed, but everything beyond 1.12 eV turns into heat. A blue photon at 2.8 eV loses more than half its energy this way. The two losses pull in opposite directions, which leads to an unavoidable trade-off.

5 · The trade-off

Why no single material catches everything

Choose a material with a smaller gap, and it absorbs more of the spectrum but loses more of each photon as heat. Choose a larger gap, and each photon is used more fully, but more photons slip through unused. Try it below, with sunlight measured as it reaches the ground.

Even the captured part can't all be delivered. A warm cell glows faintly itself, which lets some electrons and holes recombine, and that sets the cell's voltage below its gap. In 1961, William Shockley and Hans-Joachim Queisser worked out the best a single material can possibly do. The second chart shows their limit.

Silicon's gap sits close to the sweet spot. Together with its abundance and decades of manufacturing know-how, that is why the vast majority of solar panels are made of it. In practice silicon can't reach its Shockley–Queisser figure: electrons in silicon also lose energy by bumping into each other, which caps silicon at about 29%. The best laboratory silicon cells reach about 27%, and panels on roofs typically convert 20–23%.

Beating the limit. The limit applies to one material. Stack two, with a large-gap layer on top to take the high-energy photons and silicon underneath to catch the rest, and each photon is matched more closely to its gap. Such tandem cells, using perovskite on silicon, have already passed 34% in the lab.

6 · The one-way street

Separating electrons from holes

So far, our photon has made an electron–hole pair that will soon disappear. To get a current, we need a one-way street that sends electrons one way and holes the other. It is built by doping: mixing in a pinch of another element, anywhere from one atom in a thousand to one in ten million.

Phosphorus has five outer electrons, one more than its four bonds need. The spare one roams free. Silicon doped this way is called n-type, for its extra negative charges. Boron has only three, so one bond is left with a hole. That is p-type, for positive. Either kind of doped silicon is still electrically neutral overall, because every extra electron or hole is balanced by the charge of the atom that brought it.

Now put n-type and p-type silicon in contact. Near the boundary, spare electrons from the n side wander over and fill holes on the p side. That leaves a thin layer, about a micrometre wide, where the n side is slightly positive and the p side slightly negative. Those fixed charges create a strong, permanent electric field. This is the p–n junction.

On the energy ladder, the junction is a slope. Electrons roll down it to the n side. Holes behave like bubbles in water and float up it to the p side. Any pair created near the junction is pulled apart in a flash. Pairs created further away wander at random until they either reach the slope or recombine.

This is the heart of the solar cell. Light makes the pairs, and the junction's built-in slope sorts them. Electrons collect on the n side and holes on the p side. That separation of charge is a voltage, and given a wire, it drives a current.

7 · The device

Anatomy of a real cell

A real cell is a square wafer about 16–21 cm across and only about 0.15 mm thick, a little thicker than a sheet of paper. The junction lies less than a micrometre below the front surface, which is covered with a few clever layers. Pull them apart to see what each one does.

Flipped designs. This is the classic layout, with a thick p-type wafer and a thin n-type front layer. Many of today's newest cells swap the roles or add extra films that make the contacts work better, but the physics is the same: absorb, separate, collect.

8 · The plunge

How deep does a photon go?

Not every photon is absorbed at the surface. Each colour travels a typical distance into silicon before it is caught, and the distances differ enormously, so the chart needs a scale where each step is ten times the last.

Blue light is caught within a fraction of a micrometre, right next to the junction. Red light gets a few micrometres in. But near-infrared photons, which carry a large share of the sunlight that silicon can use, travel hundreds of micrometres. At 1,100 nm, that is many times the wafer's thickness.

The reason is a quirk of silicon. Its gap is indirect: an electron can't make the jump with the photon's energy alone, and needs a small nudge of momentum from the vibrating crystal at the same moment. Near the gap, that coincidence is rare, so absorption is weak.

Cells fight this with light trapping. The pyramids on the surface bend incoming light so it crosses the wafer at a slant, and the rear contact reflects it back for another pass. A ray can bounce around inside many times, which makes the wafer behave as if it were far thicker. That leaves one last problem: electrons made deep in the wafer must wander all the way back to the junction before they meet a hole. That is possible only because the crystal is so pure. In good silicon, an electron survives for up to a millisecond or so and can wander a millimetre or more.

9 · Destination

Current in the wire

Electrons collect at the front contacts and holes at the rear, leaving about 0.6–0.7 volts between them for silicon. Connect a wire from front to back through a lamp and electrons flow out of the front, through the lamp, and into the back, where each one fills a hole. The electrons aren't used up. They come back round. What the light supplies is the push.

How much power you get depends on what the cell is connected to. This model uses a standard 15.6 cm silicon cell. Change the load, and watch where the cell operates on its current–voltage curve.

At one extreme, a wire with no lamp at all (a short circuit) gives the most current, but at zero voltage, so no power. At the other, with no connection at all (an open circuit), you get the highest voltage but no current. Power is current times voltage, so it peaks at the "knee" in between, the maximum power point. Solar inverters constantly adjust their load to stay there.

Try the other sliders. Halving the sunlight halves the current, because current counts photons, but the voltage barely drops. Heat does the opposite: a hotter cell loses voltage, about 0.3–0.4% of its power for every degree. That is why panels make the most power on cold, bright days, not in the hottest weather.

One cell gives well under a volt, so panels wire 60 or more cells in series, adding their voltages to reach 30–40 volts. And the electrons in the wire? Their average drift is slower than a millimetre per second. The push that drives them, though, travels along the wire at a large fraction of the speed of light, so the lamp lights instantly.

10 · Looking back

The whole trip, on one clock

The steps of the journey happen on wildly different clocks, from a millionth of a billionth of a second to tens of thousands of years. A chart with each step ten times longer than the last fits them all on one line.

The story in five lines

  1. The Sun glows, and its light arrives as packets of energy called photons.
  2. A photon with more energy than silicon's 1.12 eV gap frees an electron and leaves a hole.
  3. Anything above the gap is lost as heat, and photons below it pass through, which limits efficiency.
  4. A p–n junction's built-in slope sends electrons to one side and holes to the other.
  5. Contacts and a wire let the electrons flow round through a load, and that flow is the current.
